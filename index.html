About

I am a PhD candidate in economics at LEMMA, Université Paris-Panthéon-Assas, supervised by Lucie Ménager and Sidartha Gordon. My dissertation, Essays in Theoretical Political Economy: Social Movements, Information, and Coordination, studies how governments use concessions and repression to manage collective mobilization, and how information — beliefs, reputation, and coordination — shapes when protest succeeds or fails.

My work sits at the intersection of game theory and political economy: signaling models, global games, and mechanism design, applied to the strategic interaction between states and citizens.

Alongside my research, I teach microeconomics at Assas and run mathematics courses at the secondary level.

Research

The Reputational Cost of Buying Peace

Dissertation chapter · working paper

A two-period signaling model of government concessions to a protest movement. Concessions operate through two channels — a belief channel, shaping what citizens infer about the government's type, and a precedent channel, shaping what future concessions cost. The model asks when a government separates itself from a tougher type by conceding, and what that reveals.

Concessions and Coordination: A Two‑Period Model of Protest Deterrence

Working paper

A two-period reduction of a protest-deterrence game — concession, then mobilization — asking how far the decision to deter a movement is pinned down by individual rationality alone once it is framed as a coordination problem, and where multiplicity of equilibria remains.

Dissertation

Essays in Theoretical Political Economy: Social Movements, Information, and Coordination — expected 2028. Additional chapters extend these models to incomplete information and to environments with many protesters, using tools from global games and population games.
